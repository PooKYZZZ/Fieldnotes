# Secret exposure

## Observation

A dummy token unexpectedly appears in a build log.

## Investigation

1. Stop sharing the affected log, artifact or screenshot. If the value could be real, treat it as compromised and follow its owner's revocation/rotation process first; deleting text does not revoke access.
2. Preserve the minimum incident metadata needed to find the run: repository, job, step, time and artifact location. Do not paste the token into search commands, issues or this learning record.
3. Review the responsible workflow and code paths for environment dumps, shell tracing, exception messages, command arguments, caches and uploaded artifacts. Use a clearly fake fixture when reproducing locally.
4. Search repository history and retained CI artifacts for the affected path/run using approved secret-scanning tools; review hits without echoing the secret value. Account for forks, downloads and logs that may already have been copied.
5. Remove the unsafe output path, verify the fake fixture is redacted, rerun the check safely, then document who will rotate/revoke a real credential and who will confirm affected copies are contained.

## Safety constraint

Work only on disposable resources or services you own, and record initial evidence before applying a
fix.

## Hint

Deleting a single line from Git history does not automatically revoke a leaked credential.

## Completion evidence

Document the affected locations and retention paths without copying the value, whether revocation/rotation was completed by the credential owner, the redaction correction, and the safe dummy-value verification. Clearly mark any artifact or copy you could not inspect.
